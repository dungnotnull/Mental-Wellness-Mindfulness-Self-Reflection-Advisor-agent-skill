# Stigma-Free Language Guide — Mental Wellbeing Education

**Purpose:** Provide guidance on using language that reduces stigma, promotes understanding, and supports mental health education. This guide ensures all communication is respectful, person-centered, and non-shaming.

> **Use this guide for:** All communications about mental health, wellbeing, and psychological experiences.

## Core Principles

1. **Person-First Language:** Put the person before the condition/experience
2. **Respectful Terminology:** Use clinically accurate, non-judgmental terms
3. **Avoid Stigma:** Challenge stereotypes and misconceptions
4. **Promote Understanding:** Use clear, accessible language
5. **Maintain Hope:** Balance realism with possibility

## Person-First Language

### Put People First

❌ **Avoid:** "Depressed person", "Anxious person", "Mentally ill person"
✅ **Use:** "Person experiencing depression", "Person with anxiety", "Person with mental health condition"

### General Principles

❌ **Avoid:** Labeling people by their experiences
✅ **Use:** Describing what people are experiencing

**Examples:**
- ❌ "She's bipolar" → ✅ "She has bipolar disorder"
- ❌ "He's an addict" → ✅ "He experiences substance use disorder"
- ❌ "They're suicidal" → ✅ "They're experiencing suicidal thoughts"

## Respectful Terminology

### Accurate Clinical Terms

| Instead Of | Use |
|-----------|-----|
| "Crazy", "Insane", "Psycho" | Specific conditions or behaviors |
| "Mental case" | Person with mental health condition |
| "Committed suicide" | Died by suicide / Completed suicide |
| "Suffering from" | Experiencing / Living with |
| "Afflicted with" | Has / Experiences |
| "Victim of" | Person who experienced / Person living with |
| "Successful suicide" | Completed suicide / Suicide death |
| "Unsuccessful suicide" | Suicide attempt / Non-fatal attempt |

### Respectful Language for Specific Conditions

**Depression:**
- ❌ "They're depressed"
- ✅ "They're experiencing depression"
- ✅ "They live with depression"

**Anxiety:**
- ❌ "They're anxious" / "They worry too much"
- ✅ "They experience anxiety"
- ✅ "They have an anxiety disorder"

**Substance Use:**
- ❌ "Addict", "Junkie", "Drug abuser"
- ✅ "Person with substance use disorder"
- ✅ "Person in recovery"

**Psychosis/Schizophrenia:**
- ❌ "Psycho", "Schizo", "Crazy"
- ✅ "Person experiencing psychosis"
- ✅ "Person with schizophrenia"

**Self-Harm/Suicide:**
- ❌ "Suicidal" (as identity)
- ✅ "Experiencing suicidal thoughts"
- ✅ "In crisis"
- ❌ "Commit suicide" (implies crime)
- ✅ "Die by suicide" / "Complete suicide"

## Non-Stigmatizing Communication

### What NOT to Say

| Phrase | Why It's Problematic |
|--------|----------------------|
| "They're crazy" | Dismissive, disrespectful |
| "It's all in their head" | Minimizes legitimate experiences |
| "They're just seeking attention" | Invalidates distress |
| "Snap out of it" | Implies simple willpower solves conditions |
| "They're off their meds" | Insensitive to medication management |
| "They're being dramatic" | Minimizes genuine distress |
| "They have a split personality" | Inaccurate stereotype about DID |

### What TO Say Instead

| Situation | Respectful Response |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Someone shares mental health struggle | "Thank you for trusting me with this" |
| Someone appears distressed | "How can I support you right now?" |
| Someone appears in crisis | "I'm here for you. You're not alone." |
| Someone discloses diagnosis | "I appreciate you sharing that with me" |
| Someone appears to be struggling | "You matter. How can I help?" |

## Challenging Stigma

### Common Stigmas and Responses

**Stigma:** "Mental health issues mean weakness"

**Response:**
"Mental health conditions are medical conditions, not character flaws. They're caused by complex factors including biology, environment, and life experiences. Seeking help is a sign of strength, not weakness."

**Stigma:** "Therapy is for people with serious problems"

**Response:**
"Therapy is like any healthcare — it's for maintenance, not just crises. Many people use therapy to prevent problems, improve relationships, and enhance wellbeing. It's a tool for everyone."

**Stigma:** "You can just think positive and feel better"

**Response:**
"Positive thinking alone doesn't resolve mental health conditions. These are legitimate medical conditions that often require professional treatment. While mindset matters, it's not the whole picture."

**Stigma:** "Medication is a crutch"

**Response:**
"Medication is one evidence-based treatment for mental health conditions. Like insulin for diabetes or medication for high blood pressure, it treats a medical condition. Using all available tools is smart, not weak."

## Hope-Balanced Language

### Balancing Realism with Hope

**Too Optimistic (Invalidating):**
"Don't worry, you'll be fine!"
"Everything happens for a reason."
"Just stay positive!"

**Too Pessimistic (Hopeless):**
"This will never get better."
"You'll always struggle with this."
There's nothing you can do."

**Balanced (Realistic Hope):**
"This is really difficult right now, and treatment can help."
Recovery is possible, and it's a journey.
"What you're experiencing is treatable."
"There are many paths forward."
"You have more strength than you know."

### Language That Supports Agency

❌ **Avoid:** Taking away agency
- "You need to..."
- "You should..."
- "You have to..."

✅ **Use:** Supporting agency
- "Some people find helpful..."
- "You might consider..."
- "What feels right to you?"
- "What options resonate for you?"

## Culture and Identity

### Culturally Responsive Language

**Principles:**
1. Acknowledge cultural differences in mental health expression
2. Respect cultural interpretations of distress
3. Avoid imposing one cultural framework
4. Recognize stigma varies across cultures
5. Use language appropriate to cultural context

**Examples:**
- "In some cultures, distress shows up physically rather than emotionally"
- "Different communities have different words for what you're experiencing"
- "Your cultural background might influence how you understand this"

### Identity-Affirming Language

**Principles:**
1. Use language consistent with people's identity
2. Respect how people identify themselves
3. Ask about preferred terminology
4. Avoid assumptions about identity

**Examples:**
- "How do you describe what you're experiencing?"
- "What language feels right for you?"
- "Is there anything about how I'm talking about this that doesn't fit for you?"

## Accessibility in Language

### Clear, Jargon-Free Communication

**Principles:**
1. Avoid clinical jargon when possible
2. Explain technical terms when used
3. Use concrete examples
4. Check for understanding
5. Adapt language to audience

**Example:**
Instead of: "You're exhibiting anhedonia"
Try: "You're noticing that things you used to enjoy don't feel as enjoyable anymore"

### Trauma-Informed Language

**Principles:**
1. Avoid graphic descriptions of trauma
2. Use language that doesn't re-traumatize
3. Focus on safety and agency
4. Avoid diagnostic language during crisis
5. Respect boundaries

**Examples:**
- ❌ "What exactly happened to you?"
- ✅ "What would be helpful to share?"
- ❌ "You're showing signs of PTSD"
- ✅ "What you're experiencing is a common response to difficult events"

## Digital Communication Guidelines

### Online/Mental Health Communication

**Principles:**
1. Assume posts will be read by many people
2. Avoid identifying information
3. Consider tone (text lacks non-verbal cues)
4. Provide crisis resources when appropriate
5. Acknowledge limitations of digital communication

**Examples:**
- "What you're sharing resonates with many people"
- "If you're in crisis, please reach out to [resources]"
- "Online support is valuable, and in-person help can be too"
- "I'm an AI, not a mental health professional"

## Crisis Communication

### Language During Crisis

**Principles:**
1. Clear, direct communication
2. Avoid judgment or shame
3. Provide concrete resources
4. Maintain hope and connection
5. Acknowledge difficulty

**Examples:**
- "I hear that you're in a lot of pain right now"
- "You're not alone, and help is available"
- "This feeling, however intense, will not last forever"
- "Your life matters, even when it doesn't feel like it"

### What to Avoid in Crisis

❌ **Avoid:**
- Dismissive language ("It's not that bad")
- Guilt-inducing language ("Think of your family")
- Minimizing ("Others have it worse")
- Judgment ("That's selfish")
- Shaming ("How could you think that?")

## Professional Communication

### When Referring to Professional Support

**Principles:**
1. Normalize help-seeking
2. Present as strength, not weakness
3. Provide concrete options
4. Respect autonomy
5. Offer ongoing support

**Examples:**
- "Many people find it helpful to talk with a professional"
- "A therapist can provide support and tools for this"
- "Would you like help finding a mental health provider?"
- "I'll stay with you while we look up resources"

### After Disclosure

**Principles:**
1. Thank the person for trusting you
2. Validate their courage
3. Ask how you can help
4. Respect boundaries
5. Offer ongoing connection

**Examples:**
- "Thank you for trusting me with this"
- "I appreciate you sharing something so personal"
- "How can I support you?"
- "You're not alone in this"

## Media Communication

### When Talking About Mental Health Publicly

**Principles:**
1. Use person-first language consistently
2. Avoid sensationalizing
3. Include recovery stories, not just crisis
4. Challenge stereotypes
5. Provide resources

**Examples:**
- Instead of "Mental illness epidemic" → "Many people experience mental health conditions"
- Instead of "Suffering from depression" → "Living with depression"
- Instead of "Committed suicide" → "Died by suicide"

## Quality Checklist

Before communicating about mental health, verify:

- [ ] Person-first language used
- [ ] Clinical terms accurate and respectful
- [ ] No stigmatizing language present
- [ ] Tone conveys respect and hope
- [ ] Avoids minimization or invalidation
- [ ] Cultural context considered
- [ ] Jargon explained or avoided
- [ ] Agency maintained
- [ ] Crisis resources included if relevant
- [ ] Professional boundaries clear

## Common Mistakes

### Mistake: "They're So Strong/BRAVE"

**Issue:** Can feel like pressure or invalidation

**Alternative:** "I appreciate you sharing this with me" / "This sounds really difficult"

### Mistake: "I Understand Exactly What You're Going Through"

**Issue:** You don't, actually

**Alternative:** "I can't imagine exactly what this is like for you"

### Mistake: "You'll Get Over It"

**Issue:** Dismissive and unrealistic

**Alternative:** "This is really hard right now. Support can help"

### Mistake: "At Least It's Not [Worse Thing]"

**Issue:** Invalidates current struggle

**Alternative:** "What you're experiencing sounds really difficult"

## Ongoing Learning

**Stay Updated:**
- Language evolves (stigma reduction advances)
- Communities have preferences
- Research informs best practices
- Individual preferences vary

**When Unsure:**
- Ask: "How would you like me to talk about this?"
- Default to respect and clarity
- Prioritize the person's preference
- Acknowledge what you don't know
- Commit to learning
